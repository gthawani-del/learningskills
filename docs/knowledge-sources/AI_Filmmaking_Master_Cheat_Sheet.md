## 1. How to Direct AI Actors: The Performance Cheat Sheet to Save

### Why AI Acting Feels Fake
* ❌ Vague prompts like "natural emotion"
* ❌ Too many subtle actions in one short clip
* ❌ No clear trigger, timing, or end state
* ❌ Random gestures instead of motivated behavior
* 💡 **Rule:** Don't prompt the emotion. Direct the behavior that reveals it.

### The Performance Formula
1. **Attention:** What the character is focused on
2. **Trigger:** The exact word / object / action that changes them
3. **Main Reaction:** The one behavior that matters most
4. **Secondary Behavior:** One supporting detail only
5. **Timing:** Pause, beat, or delay
6. **End State:** Where the actor settles

> **Core Rule:** 1 clip = 1 main beat + 1 secondary behavior.

### Use This Prompt Structure
`[SHOT]. [WHO + CONTEXT]. [ATTENTION TARGET]. [TRIGGER]. [MAIN REACTION]. [ONE SECONDARY BEHAVIOR]. [TIMING]. [END STATE]. Keep movement restrained.`
* Start simple. Add one new behavior at a time.

### 8 Micro-Performance Techniques

| Technique | What It Is | When To Use | Prompt Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Direct the Eyes** | Eyes should track attention, not stare blankly. | Conversations, reveals, inner conflict. | *Medium close-up. She listens to him, eyes mostly on his face. At the word 'father,' her gaze flicks to the photo frame, then returns to him.* |
| **Blink Timing** | Blink at natural beat changes, not on a fixed loop. | Pauses, sentence endings, thought shifts. | *Locked medium close-up. He listens without constant blinking. After she finishes the sentence, he pauses, gives one small natural blink, then looks down.* |
| **Real Listening** | The silent character is still processing the line. | Dialogue scenes, interrogations, emotional reveals. | *Medium close-up on the listener. She watches him closely, gives one small acknowledging nod midway, then her brow tightens slightly before he finishes.* |
| **Breath + Hesitation** | Breathing and pauses reveal tension before words do. | Confessions, fear, hard decisions, vulnerable scenes. | *Tight medium shot. Her lips part, but she stops. She takes a small inhale, looks at the floor, then answers after a short pause.* |
| **Weight Shift** | Movement begins with balance, not sliding feet. | Standing starts, exits, confrontations, nervous pauses. | *Full-body shot. Before walking away, he settles his weight onto his left leg, lifts the right heel, then steps forward naturally.* |
| **Hands Tell the Truth** | Hands should have a specific job, not random gestures. | Tension, restraint, guilt, concentration. | *Seated at the table. Her left hand stays around the cup. As the question becomes personal, her right thumb presses against her index finger once.* |
| **Expressions Have Time** | Real expressions build, peak, and fade. | Subtle sadness, shock, disappointment, quiet realization. | *Her smile weakens first, then her eyes lose focus for a moment, and only after that does her brow tighten slightly. Keep the change gradual.* |
| **Use Stillness Intentionally** | Stillness can be stronger than constant motion. | Close-ups, pressure, silent conflict, emotional restraint. | *After hearing the accusation, he stays almost perfectly still for two seconds. Only his eyes shift and his jaw tightens slightly before he answers.* |

### The Strongest Rule
* **Principle:** Don't ask AI for feelings. Direct what the character notices, how the body reacts, and when that reaction happens.
* **Weak:** *"She reacts naturally."*
* **Better:** *"She hears his last sentence, pauses, gives one blink, lowers her gaze, then answers without lifting her head."*

### What Not To Do
1. "Natural acting" with no specific behavior.
2. 6 subtle actions in a 5-second clip.
3. Random hand gestures every sentence.
4. Perfectly mirrored poses and expressions.
5. Blink loops and constant nodding.
6. Emotion words without a trigger or timing.

## 2. 10 Composition Techniques Every AI Filmmaker Should Know

### 1. Why Composition Matters
* Guides the audience's attention.
* Creates depth and visual balance.
* Supports character and story.
* Gives movement and purpose.
* Makes shots feel intentional and cinematic.

### 2. Quick Composition Groups
* **Subject Placement:** Rule of Thirds, Center Composition, Negative Space
* **Frame Geometry & Depth:** Symmetry, Leading Lines, Frame Within a Frame, Foreground–Midground–Background, Asymmetrical Balance
* **People & Motion:** Character Blocking, Composition for Movement

### The 10 Composition Techniques

| # | Technique | What It Is | When To Use It | Visual Placement | AI Prompt Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **Rule of Thirds** | Place the subject near a third line or intersection, not center. | Create balance and open space in the direction of gaze. | Subject on a third line or point. | *Wide shot of a hiker on the left third, looking right toward a sunset over mountains.* |
| **2** | **Center Composition** | Place the subject in the center of the frame. | Emphasize importance, power, or stillness. | Subject centered on both axes. | *Portrait of a woman centered in a grand hallway, symmetrical composition, cinematic lighting.* |
| **3** | **Negative Space** | Use empty space around the subject. | Show isolation, scale, or contemplation. | Subject occupies a small portion of the frame. | *Wide shot of a person on a cliff with vast empty sky at sunset.* |
| **4** | **Symmetry** | Balance elements around a central axis. | Create order, harmony, or a formal mood. | Subject and environment mirrored on both sides. | *Wide shot of a person centered in a symmetrical archway, reflections on the floor.* |
| **5** | **Leading Lines** | Use lines in the environment to guide the eye toward the subject. | Lead the viewer's attention and create depth. | Subject at the end of converging lines. | *Wide shot of a person walking on a bridge with leading lines converging toward them, city skyline at sunset.* |
| **6** | **Frame Within a Frame** | Use a foreground structure to frame the subject. | Add depth, context, or focus to the subject. | Subject inside a frame (door, window, arch, etc.). | *Shot through an archway framing a person looking at a cityscape in the distance.* |
| **7** | **Foreground–Midground–Background** | Use multiple layers to create depth. | Make the scene feel immersive and three-dimensional. | Foreground + subject + background. | *Wide shot with foreground rocks, a hiker in the midground, and snow-capped mountains in the background.* |
| **8** | **Asymmetrical Balance** | Balance different visual weights without mirroring. | Create visual tension, mood, or contrast. | Large element balanced by a smaller element. | *Wide shot of a seated man on the left in shadow, balanced by a small figure in a bright doorway on the right.* |
| **9** | **Character Blocking** | Position multiple characters intentionally. | Show relationships, hierarchy, or conflict. | Characters arranged in a triangle or clear positions. | *Three characters in a triangular arrangement, two seated in front and one standing behind, moody cinematic lighting.* |
| **10** | **Composition for Movement** | Leave space in front of the subject to show movement. | Make the movement feel natural and dynamic. | Subject on one side with open space in the direction of movement. | *Wide shot of a runner on the left with open space ahead, running toward the right, city skyline in the background.* |

### 11. Common Composition Mistakes
* Centering everything by default.
* Ignoring empty space.
* Flattening the background.
* Using shallow depth of field instead of depth.
* Forgetting movement direction.
* Writing vague or generic prompts.

### 12. Prompt It Visually
`Subject placement` + `Eye-line direction` + `Empty space` + `Depth layers` + `Lines / geometry` + `Character relationship` + `Movement direction`
> **Motto:** Compose the frame, not just the subject.

## 3. How to Cut a Scene Like an AI Filmmaker (Part 1 — Why, When, and Where to Cut)

*Great editing is not random shot-switching. A cut should happen when the audience needs a new piece of information, emotion, or perspective.*

### 1. The Simplest Decision (Always ask: HOLD, MOVE, or CUT?)
1. **HOLD:** Stay in the shot while it is still showing the most important thing.
   * *Best for:* Performance, tension, observation.
   * *Effect:* Patience, weight, realism.
2. **MOVE:** Move the camera when the same moment should continue, but attention needs to shift or intensify.
   * *Best for:* Reveals, rising emotion, discovery.
   * *Effect:* Continuity, immersion.
3. **CUT:** Cut when another viewpoint can tell the next part of the story better.
   * *Best for:* New information, reaction, detail, geography.
   * *Effect:* Clarity, rhythm, emphasis.

### 2. What Usually Happens Inside a Shot?
* **SETUP** (The situation is established) $\rightarrow$ **CHANGE** (Something new happens) $\rightarrow$ **RESPONSE** (A character or the scene reacts).
* *Note:* Many good cuts happen after the meaningful change lands.

### 3. When Is It Time to Cut?
* **A. Cut before the reaction:** Creates anticipation. The audience waits for the response.
  * *Example:* We hear the door click, then cut to her face before she reacts.
* **B. Cut during the reaction:** Creates flow and energy. Motion carries the cut.
  * *Example:* She turns toward the sound, and the next shot continues the same movement.
* **C. Cut after the reaction:** Creates weight and tension. We feel the moment before leaving it.
  * *Example:* She freezes, breathes, then we cut.
* *Rule:* **There is no single magical frame.** Cut based on what you want the audience to feel: anticipation, impact, or aftermath.

### 4. What Should Happen Before You Cut? (Checklist)
* [ ] The current shot has delivered its key information.
* [ ] A new question has been created.
* [ ] Another view would now be more useful.
* [ ] The actor's movement, emotion, or eyeline motivates the next shot.
* [ ] The next shot continues the story, not just the coverage.

### 5. A Simple Scene Example
1. **[Wide Shot]:** Woman beside the table. Understand the room. $\rightarrow$
2. **[Medium Close-Up]:** The handle clicks. She begins turning toward the door. $\rightarrow$
3. **[Insert Shot of Door Handle]:** Cut when the new focus becomes more important than the current shot.

## 4. Camera Lenses, Explained for AI Filmmakers
*What each setting MEANS • What it CHANGES • What to PROMPT*

### Key Things to Remember
1. Focal length changes framing, not perspective (camera position does).
2. Smaller f-number = bigger opening = blurrier background.
3. Anamorphic $\neq$ automatically blue flares.
4. Shutter controls motion blur.
5. Lens terms in AI prompts are visual cues, not guaranteed physical settings.

### 1. Focal Length = How Wide the Camera Sees
*The lens measurement in millimeters (mm) [Full-Frame Reference: 36mm wide sensor]. On the same sensor, a smaller number captures more of the scene; a larger number captures less.*
* **24mm | Wide, more room:** Shows more of the environment.
* **50mm | Medium view:** Natural, everyday look.
* **85mm | Narrow, closer-looking framing:** Less environment, subject looks larger.
* **IMPORTANT:** Changing focal length alone does NOT change perspective. It only changes how much of the scene is in the frame. To change perspective (e.g., make background look bigger or smaller), you need to move the camera closer or farther from the subject.

### 2. Aperture / F-Stop = Size of Lens Opening
*The f-number ($f/1.8$, $f/4$, $f/8$, etc.) tells you how wide the lens opening is. A smaller f-number = bigger opening = more light = shallower depth of field (blurrier background), assuming the same camera position and framing.*
* **$f/1.8$ | Blurred background:** Shallow depth of field. Subject stands out.
* **$f/8$ | More background sharp:** Deeper depth of field. More of the scene is in focus.
* **DEPTH OF FIELD** = how much of the distance in a scene looks acceptably sharp.

### 3. Spherical vs Anamorphic
*Spherical lenses capture a normal image with no horizontal squeeze. Anamorphic lenses squeeze the image horizontally in-camera and it is desqueezed for viewing. They can produce distinct visual traits.*
* **Spherical:** Natural, clean look; round bokeh highlights; no horizontal squeeze.
* **Anamorphic:** Wider field of view (for a given height); often oval bokeh highlights; can have horizontal lens flares; many different looks (subtle or strong).
* *Note:* These are possible lens characteristics, not guarantees for every lens. Cropping to 2.39:1 does NOT make footage anamorphic.

### 4. Focus, Shutter & Grain = How Motion and Sharpness Look
* **Focus Pulling (Rack Focus):** A deliberate shift of sharpness from one subject to another, without zooming or moving the camera (e.g., *1. Foreground object sharp, scientist blurred* $\rightarrow$ *2. Scientist sharp, object blurred*).
* **Shutter Speed / Shutter Angle:** Controls how long each frame is exposed, which affects motion blur. At 24 fps, a $180^\circ$ shutter = $1/48$ second.
  * *$90^\circ$ ($1/96\text{s}$):* Less motion blur. Sharper, more crisp.
  * *$180^\circ$ ($1/48\text{s}$):* More motion blur. Smoother, more cinematic.
* **Film Grain vs Sensor Noise:**
  * *Film grain (organic, textured):* The natural, organic texture from photographic film.
  * *Digital sensor noise (more electronic):* Different from film grain and usually looks more electronic.

### 5. Copy These 3 AI Shot Prompts
* **WIDE / 24mm:** *Wide shot of a scientist in a laboratory. Camera at eye level. Show the scientist, workbench, and chamber. Keep the room readable with deep focus.*
* **CLOSE / 85mm:** *Close-up of the same scientist from farther away. Eyes critically sharp; distant chamber softly blurred. Natural portrait perspective.*
* **RACK FOCUS:** *Locked camera. Start with the foreground specimen sharp and the scientist blurred. Smoothly shift focus to the scientist's eyes. No zoom or camera movement.*

## 7. AI Filmmaking: The Beginner Cheat Sheet
*A practical guide to tools, prompting, references, workflow, and craft.*

### 01. Start With the Story
* **Define the outcome:** Short film, ad, trailer, music video, explainer, or concept scene.
* **Choose the format:** 16:9, 9:16, square, or widescreen.
* **Decide the genre:** Tone, audience, and emotional goal.
* **Write a one-line premise** before generating anything.

### 02. Choose Your Tool Stack
* **Writing & planning:** Premise, beat sheet, shot list, storyboard.
* **Image generation:** Characters, locations, props, style frames.
* **Video generation:** Shot creation, motion tests, iterations.
* **Voice / music / sound:** Dialogue, narration, ambience, score, SFX.
* **Editing / finishing:** Assembly, pacing, subtitles, color, export.
* *Note:* You do not need the best tool in every category. You need a stable workflow.

### 03. Build Strong References
* **Character sheet:** Front, side, close-up, costume, expression range.
* **Location sheet:** Layout, landmarks, lighting, camera zones.
* **Prop sheet:** Key objects, materials, scale, wear, damage state.
* **Style sheet:** Color palette, texture, camera language, mood.
* **Continuity sheet:** Where one shot ends and the next begins.
* *Tip:* Good references reduce drift and improve consistency.

### 04. Prompt Like a Filmmaker
* **Structured Shot Prompt:** `Subject` $\rightarrow$ `Action` $\rightarrow$ `Shot size` $\rightarrow$ `Lens` $\rightarrow$ `Camera move` $\rightarrow$ `Lighting` $\rightarrow$ `Environment` $\rightarrow$ `Performance` $\rightarrow$ `Continuity / End state`
* **Example:** *Medium tracking shot, 35mm. A tired detective walks through a rainy alley. Handheld camera follows from screen-left. Cold sodium-vapor street light, reflective ground, shallow depth. He checks behind him, anxious, breathing hard. End with him stopping at a flickering doorway.*
* *Tip:* Think in shots, not paragraphs.

### 05. Generate Shot by Shot
* Work one shot at a time, not paragraph by paragraph.
* Lock identity, wardrobe, environment, and direction of movement.
* Use first-frame and last-frame thinking for continuity.
* Keep every shot motivated: what changed, what did we learn?
* Iterate small: fix one issue at a time.

### 06. Learn the Core Craft
* **Story:** Stakes, conflict, progression.
* **Framing:** Shot size, composition, lens feel.
* **Lighting:** Mood, motivation, contrast.
* **Performance:** Gesture, timing, eye-line, emotion.
* **Editing & sound:** Rhythm, tension, payoff.
* *Note:* AI images alone are not cinema. Sequencing, emotion, and intent turn them into film.

### 07. Follow a Simple Workflow
1. **Premise:** One-line idea $\rightarrow$
2. **Beat sheet:** Beginning, middle, end $\rightarrow$
3. **References:** Characters, locations, props, style $\rightarrow$
4. **Shot list:** Shots, angles, actions $\rightarrow$
5. **Generate visuals:** Create and iterate $\rightarrow$
6. **Edit + sound:** Pacing, color, music, SFX $\rightarrow$
7. **Review + refine:** Check consistency and improve.
* *Rule:* Start simple and build consistency before scale.

### 08. Avoid Beginner Mistakes
* Starting with visuals before story.
* Overloading prompts with random adjectives.
* Ignoring screen direction and geography.
* Changing style, costume, or lighting mid-scene.
* Making every shot epic instead of meaningful.
* Neglecting sound design and pacing.

## 8. Color Theory for AI Filmmakers
*Practical, cinematic color strategies to create consistent, beautiful and meaningful visuals. Color shapes mood, guides attention, separates your character from the environment and keeps your entire film visually consistent.*

### 1. The Building Blocks of Color
* **Hue (Color Family):** The actual color (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, etc.).
* **Saturation (Intensity):** How pure or vibrant the color is. High saturation looks bold; low saturation looks muted. *Use low saturation for cinematic realism and high saturation for controlled accents.*
* **Luminance (Brightness):** How light or dark the color is. It controls contrast and what the viewer notices first. *Brighter areas attract attention. Use luminance to separate your character from the background.*

### 2. Warm vs Cool Colors
* **Warm Colors:** Often include red, orange, yellow and some warm neutrals. They can create energy, intimacy and a sense of warmth or danger.
  * *Example (Warm interior / tungsten light):* *An intimate interior scene with a warm amber and brown palette, soft tungsten lighting, low saturation, cinematic look.*
* **Cool Colors:** Often include blue, cyan, green and cool neutrals. They can create calm, tension, isolation or a modern, clinical feel.
  * *Example (Cool exterior / moonlight):* *A night street scene with a cool blue-gray palette, low saturation, soft moonlight, cinematic atmosphere.*

### 3. Complementary Colors
*Colors opposite each other on the color wheel create strong contrast and make your subject stand out. Use them to separate the character from the environment.*
* **Orange $\leftrightarrow$ Blue:** Warm character, cool environment. Classic cinematic contrast.
  * *Prompt:* *A character in a warm orange tungsten light against a cool blue night background.*
* **Red $\leftrightarrow$ Green:** Strong separation and visual impact. Use sparingly.
  * *Prompt:* *A woman in a dark red coat in a desaturated green environment, cinematic mood.*
* **Yellow $\leftrightarrow$ Purple:** Creates a stylized, dramatic look.
  * *Prompt:* *A close-up with warm yellow light on the face and a soft purple background, cinematic lighting.*

### 4. How Saturation Changes the Mood
* **Low Saturation:** Feels realistic and cinematic; good for drama, thriller, realism; keeps focus on story and character.
  * *Prompt:* *A desaturated, realistic palette with muted colors, low saturation, cinematic grain.*
* **Medium Saturation:** Natural and balanced; works for most genres; looks intentional and clean.
  * *Prompt:* *A natural cinematic palette with moderate saturation, balanced colors.*
* **High Saturation (Accent):** Creates strong visual impact; use for key elements only; too much can look artificial.
  * *Prompt:* *A dark scene with low overall saturation, with only the red neon sign highly saturated.*

### 5. Character vs Environment Color
* **Stand Out (Contrast):** Different color family; character grabs attention; good for key scenes.
  * *Prompt:* *A woman in a red scarf in a cool, desaturated blue-gray city environment.*
* **Belong (Harmonize):** Similar color families; feels part of the world; good for natural or subtle scenes.
  * *Prompt:* *A woman in brown clothing in a warm earthy environment with similar tones, cinematic look.*
* **Feel Different (Isolated):** One color element breaks the palette; creates tension or alienation; useful for specific story moments.
  * *Prompt:* *A woman in a neutral white outfit in a blue-gray environment, feeling isolated and out of place.*

### 6. Build a Color Palette for Your Film
* **Sample Palette:** Dominant 1 (*Charcoal*), Dominant 2 (*Muted Olive*), Dominant 3 (*Warm Beige*), Accent (*Rust Red*).
* **Palette Tips:**
  * Use 2–3 dominant colors + 1 accent color.
  * Keep skin tones natural.
  * Stay consistent across scenes (e.g., *City, Interior, Lab, Desert*).
  * Adjust palette by location or act.
  * Use reference images for consistency.

### 7. Color Script: Evolve the Palette with the Story
* **Act 1 — Introduction:** Warmer, more saturated $\rightarrow$
* **Act 2 — Conflict:** Cooler, less saturated $\rightarrow$
* **Act 3 — Low Point:** Dark and desaturated $\rightarrow$
* **Act 4 — Climax:** High contrast, strong accent $\rightarrow$
* **Ending:** Return or contrast.

### 8. Ready-to-Use Prompt Templates
* **Prompt Structure:** `[Scene description], [dominant color palette], [saturation level], [lighting (warm/cool + source)], [character vs environment], [accent color and where it appears], [mood and style].`
* **Thriller (Night):** *"A moody night street with cool blue-gray tones, low saturation, warm orange streetlights, the woman in a red scarf as the only saturated element, cinematic."*
* **Sci-Fi (Lab):** *"A futuristic laboratory with desaturated gray and blue tones, low saturation, controlled lighting, small amber indicators, cinematic look."*
* **Drama (Interior):** *"An intimate interior scene with warm brown and beige palette, moderate saturation, soft tungsten lighting, natural skin tones, cinematic."*

### 9. Image-to-Video: Keep the Colors Consistent
1. **Generate a still image** (sets the colors, composition, and style).
2. **Use it as the starting frame.**
3. **Prompt the motion (not the colors):** e.g., *"She looks around and walks forward as the camera slowly pushes in."*

### 10. Common Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)
| ❌ Mistake | ✔️ Fix |
| :--- | :--- |
| Using generic prompts like "cinematic colors" | Specify the actual palette, saturation and lighting |
| Too many strong colors in one scene | Use 2–3 dominant colors and 1 accent |
| Character blends into background | Adjust color and/or brightness for separation |
| Inconsistent palettes across scenes | Use reference images and a simple color plan |
| Relying only on a LUT to fix everything | Get the color right during generation, then refine in post |
| Overly saturated AI look | Keep saturation controlled and realistic |
| Ignoring lighting and luminance | Define what should be brighter or darker |
| No clear accent color | Choose one key accent and use it intentionally |

## 9. How to Prompt Like an AI Filmmaker
*A practical guide to writing cinematic prompts for realistic AI video and image generation.*

### 1. The Filmmaker Prompt Framework
1. **INTENT:** What should the audience notice, feel or understand in this shot? $\rightarrow$
2. **SUBJECT + ACTION:** Who/what is in the shot, exact action, blocking and direction. $\rightarrow$
3. **FRAME:** Shot size, angle, height, composition, subject placement, foreground / background. $\rightarrow$
4. **OPTICS:** Focal length, depth of field, perspective and distortion. $\rightarrow$
5. **CAMERA:** Type of camera move and why. (Choose one motivated move). $\rightarrow$
6. **PERFORMANCE + PHYSICS:** Expression, eye-line, body movement, momentum, object interaction, realistic behavior. $\rightarrow$
7. **LIGHT + SPACE:** Light source, direction, mood, environment and only relevant details. $\rightarrow$
8. **CONTINUITY:** Start state, end state, identity, costume, props, screen direction, location consistency.

### 2. Shot Language Quick Reference (Additional Terms)
*(Deduplicated against Slide 2 & Slide 5)*
* **Additional Shot Sizes:**
  * *Extreme close-up:* Tight detail and tension.
  * *Insert shot:* Key object or detail that matters.
* **Additional Camera Angles & Heights:**
  * *Eye level:* Natural and neutral.
  * *Ground level:* Extreme perspective.
  * *Shoulder height:* Common for dialogue.
  * *Overhead:* Shows layout and movement.
  * *Profile / side:* Shows direction and shape.
  * *Three-quarter:* More depth and dimension.

### 3. Lens Choices (Additional Option)
*(24mm, 50mm, 85mm, and Macro deduplicated against Slides 4 & 6)*
* **35mm:** Natural and versatile, lively and immersive spatial feel.

### 4. Camera Movement (and When to Use It)
*Rule: Use one motivated camera move per shot. Don't add movement just because it sounds cinematic.*
* **Static:** Observation, control, tension.
* **Push-in:** Increase attention or pressure.
* **Pull-back:** Reveal context or isolation.
* **Tracking:** Follows subject through space.
* **Pan / Tilt:** Reveal or redirect attention.
* **Handheld:** Subjective, urgent, unstable.
* **Crane / Orbit:** Show scale or relationship.
* **Rack focus:** Shift attention between planes.

### 5. Text-to-Video vs Image-to-Video Prompting
* **Text-to-Video (describe everything):** Use this when starting from text only.
  * `[FRAME + OPTICS] of [SUBJECT + ACTION] in [SPACE]. [CAMERA]. [PERFORMANCE + PHYSICS]. [LIGHT + MATERIAL]. [CONTINUITY / END STATE].`
* **Image-to-Video (focus on motion):** The image already defines subject, style and composition. Do not waste prompt tokens re-describing what the image already controls.
  * `[CAMERA MOTION] as [SUBJECT ACTION]. [PERFORMANCE + SCENE MOTION + TIMING]. [CONTINUITY / END STATE].`

### 6. Weak Prompt $\rightarrow$ Directed Prompt
* **Weak Prompt:** *"A scared man runs through a dark street. Cinematic, realistic, 4K."*
  * *What's missing?* Shot size and angle, lens choice, camera movement, performance direction, lighting logic, environment details, screen direction, start and end state.
* **Directed Cinematic Prompt:** *"Medium-wide, chest-height 35mm tracking shot. A soaked man runs screen-left to screen-right through a rain-slick alley, glancing back over his left shoulder. Camera tracks backward at his pace with slight handheld instability. Hard breathing, tense jaw, shoulders tight; his right shoe briefly loses traction and his coat reacts to speed. Warm sodium light from camera-right, cool ambient rain, wet asphalt with reflections. Start as he bursts from shadow. End as he brakes beside a locked steel door, still oriented screen-right."*

### 7. Performance & Physical Realism
*Direct believable human behavior, not just actions. Include details like:*
* Facial expression, eye movement, blinking
* Breathing, hesitation, timing
* Posture, weight shift, balance, foot contact
* Clothing, hair and object interaction
* Momentum, gravity and environmental response

### 8. Lighting, Environment and Materials
* Motivated light source and direction
* Soft or hard light, contrast, exposure
* Practical lights (lamps, windows, neon, etc.)
* Only describe environment details that matter
* Include realistic materials and textures (skin pores, fabric, dust, metal, scratches, etc.)

### 9. Continuity Matters
* Same character identity and outfit
* Same location and geography
* Consistent lighting and time of day
* Maintain screen direction and eyelines
* Track props, injuries and damage
* Define exact end state of this shot to match the next shot

### 10. One Shot, One Beat
* 5–10 seconds = one main action or simple progression.
* Don't cram multiple camera setups, actions or location changes.
* A cut is an edit, not a camera move.
* If you need a sequence in one shot, use clear order: *first X, then Y, finally Z.*

### 11. Common Prompting Mistakes
* ❌ Generic buzzwords ("cinematic, epic, 8K")
* ❌ Conflicting camera motions or instructions in a short shot
* ❌ No performance direction
* ❌ Ignoring physics and object interaction
* ❌ Ignoring scale and proportions
* ❌ Random production design clutter
* ❌ Unmotivated lighting changes
* ❌ Forgetting screen direction
* ❌ Inconsistent with previous shot
* ❌ Re-describing image-to-video references

### 12. Pro Iteration Order & What NOT to Over-Specify
* **Change one variable at a time:**
  1. Action first (subject and clear goal)
  2. Camera second (frame, lens, movement)
  3. Performance + physics
  4. Light + environment
  5. Continuity + end state
* **What NOT to over-specify:**
  * Camera brand names
  * Every composition rule in one prompt
  * Unnecessary technical jargon
  * Details already defined by your reference image

## 10. 8 Camera Angles Every AI Filmmaker Should Master (Excerpt: #3 & #4)
*What each angle communicates, when to use it, and how to prompt it like a director.*

* **Prompt Formula:** `SHOT SIZE` + `CAMERA HEIGHT` + `ANGLE` + `LENS` + `COMPOSITION` + `CAMERA BEHAVIOR` + `ACTION`
* **💡 Rule:** Choose an angle for dramatic purpose, not because it looks cinematic.

### 3. Ground-Level (Scale / Speed / Physicality)
* **What it is:** Camera placed near the floor or pavement.
* **What it does:** Exaggerates foreground scale, speed and physical contact.
* **Best for:** Vehicles, creatures, footsteps, size contrast.
* **Scene example:** A tiny getaway jeep tears past towering pedestrians.
* **AI prompt:** *Ground-level wide shot, camera 15 cm above asphalt, 24mm lens. A tiny jeep races screen-left to screen-right through the foreground while pedestrians tower in the background. Wheels bounce over cracks; loose paper reacts to airflow.*
* **🔖 Best use in AI:** Use camera height to reinforce scale.

### 4. High Angle (Vulnerability / Exposure / Entrapment)
* **What it is:** Camera above the subject, looking downward.
* **What it does:** Makes the subject feel smaller, exposed or controlled by space.
* **Best for:** Fear, isolation, trap reveals, tactical geography.
* **Scene example:** A fugitive realizes every exit is closing.
* **AI prompt:** *High-angle wide shot from 4 metres above the corridor, 35mm lens. The fugitive stops as three security doors close around him. Keep him small in the lower center of frame with empty floor surrounding him.*
* **🔖 Best use in AI:** Show more floor and geometry than face.
